> I'm skeptical of frameworks that work perfectly in theory. I'm interested in what holds when conditions are bad, resources are limited, and you can't rely on consistency.

## What bad conditions means

Not the power cut. Not the internet drop. Those are brief situations. They pass in hours.

Bad conditions are what doesn't pass: people who don't communicate, circumstances that shift overnight, decisions other people make that land on your operation without warning. A client moves a deadline. A team member goes silent on a critical task. A partner changes scope after the work started.

You can't control any of it. You can only build systems that don't collapse when it happens.

## The test

One question:

**Does this still work if everyone around me fails to cooperate?**

If it depends on the team being disciplined, it's a hope.
If it depends on me remembering to check it, it's a hope.
If it depends on the client respecting scope, it's a hope.
If it depends on my brain showing up on schedule, it's a hope.

Hopes are not systems. They work once, then need me to be a better version of myself 200 more times. That math doesn't work.

## What holds instead

The system that doesn't need the favorable input to function.

- **Remote ops**: the report surfaces problems. It doesn't wait for people to raise their hand.
- **ADHD**: tasks live where the decision happens. The next action is there without memory or motivation.
- **The clinic**: the hire makes taking work back harder than letting it go. Behavior follows structure.
- **Strategy**: the position holds when execution is mediocre. If it vanishes on a bad week, it was never a position.

One test, applied four ways: strip the favorable condition, see what's still standing.

## The principle

**Build for the bad day.** If it holds when people fail and circumstances shift, it'll more than hold when they don't. The reverse is never true.

That's the whole test. It's the only one that's never let me down.
