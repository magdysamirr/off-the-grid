Blame culture is what you get when teaching is replaced by verdicts.

The pattern is easy to spot. A senior person does not teach, does not share context, does not warn. They wait. When the predictable mistake arrives, they perform the correction. The junior is blamed for failing a test they did not know they were taking. The senior walks away feeling like a manager. Nobody got better.

## Why it feels like standards

Blame masquerades as accountability. The senior who punishes believes they are enforcing the standard. They are not. They are performing authority at someone else's expense.

The difference between a standard and a verdict is simple: a standard was known in advance, a verdict was not. If the person did not know the rule, the test, the expectation, then punishing the failure is not enforcement. It is a trap.

## What it actually costs

The first cost is the obvious one: the person blamed learns to hide, not to fix. Nobody surfaces a problem into a culture that punishes problems. So small issues stop being reported, and small issues become big ones in silence.

The second cost is the one people miss. The team watches what management tolerates. If the senior can skip teaching and still hold court, the lesson travels: hiding your failures is safer than surfacing them. Responsibility becomes transferable. Someone else is expected to absorb the damage. That is how culture changes without a meeting.

## The test

Before you call something accountability, ask: did they know the rule before they broke it? Was the expectation stated in advance? Was the teaching offered while there was still time to use it?

If the answer is no to any of these, you are not enforcing standards. You are [[The Trap, The Tempter, and The Mercy Clause|setting a trap]]. And the person falling into it is the one you failed, not the one who failed you.

The fix is not softer management. It is honest management: teach first, then hold to the standard you actually taught.
